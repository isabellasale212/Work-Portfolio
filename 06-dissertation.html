Does jump-test power transfer to change-of-direction braking?

A force-plate study of 20 university athletes testing whether eccentric rate of force development (RFD) from jump tests reflects the braking demands of a 180° change of direction — and whether it predicts change-of-direction speed.

Award
BSc (Hons) Sport & Exercise Science
Institution
University of Bath
Supervisor
Dr Adam Brazil
Sample
n = 20 · force plates 2000 Hz

The question

Change-of-direction (COD) speed is a decisive quality in multidirectional sport, and the deceleration — the eccentric “braking” — is its hardest phase. Practitioners routinely profile athletes with jump tests and assume the qualities transfer. Do they?

I tested whether eccentric RFD from three jumps — countermovement (CMJ), drop (DJ) and broad (BJ) — reflects the eccentric braking RFD measured during a 505 change-of-direction task, and whether those jump qualities relate to 505 performance.

Method

  • 20 BUCS athletes (age 21 ± 1.3 yr, 1.78 ± 0.07 m, 74.1 ± 13.6 kg).
  • 3D motion capture (Qualisys, 200 Hz) with force plates (2000 Hz), modelled in Visual3D — RFD computed as ΔForce / ΔTime from the vertical force trace.
  • Four tasks: CMJ, DJ, BJ and a 505 COD, with a best-trial selection criterion (CMJ jump height, DJ RSI, BJ distance, fastest 505).
  • Statistics: Pearson / Spearman correlations, independent t-tests / Mann–Whitney for fast-vs-slow groups, and repeated-measures ANOVA for task differences in RFD.

Results

One jump quality related to COD performance: a significant negative correlation between CMJ peak RFD and 505 time — faster athletes produced force more rapidly. But no jump-derived RFD reflected the 505’s braking RFD, and RM-ANOVA showed large, task-specific differences in RFD.

Conclusion & implication

What it means for practice

Eccentric RFD is a non-transferable quality. Practitioners should profile deceleration with COD-specific force-plate braking metrics — such as horizontal braking impulse — rather than inferring it from jump tests.

The result matters because it challenges a common shortcut in athlete testing: that a countermovement jump can stand in for change-of-direction braking. It can’t, and the data says so cleanly.
